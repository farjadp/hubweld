CWB certification is a welder qualification issued by the Canadian Welding Bureau under CSA W47.1 (steel), W47.2 (aluminum), W186 (rebar) or W55.3 (resistance welding). You get it by passing a practical test witnessed by a CWB representative, either through a CWB-certified employer, an accredited test centre or a union hall. Most tickets last two years, and test centres charge roughly $170–$185 per position.

Key takeaways

  • A CWB ticket is process- and position-specific. A 3G FCAW ticket says nothing about your ability to stick weld pipe.
  • There are three routes to a test: in-house with a CWB-certified company, at a CWB Accredited Test Centre, or through a CWB Accredited Union Hall.
  • The test plate is cut into bend specimens. For steel plate under CSA W47.1 that means two root bends and one face bend, bent through 180°.
  • Most qualifications are valid for two years if you keep working for a certified company, with a check test window from 90 days before to 90 days after expiry.
  • CWB does not set a national test fee. Published 2026 prices run about $170–$185 per position, with some centres capping a four-position package at $720.
  • A CWB ticket and a Red Seal are different credentials. Many employers ask for both.

What is CWB certification and who needs it?

CWB certification is proof that you, as an individual welder, have produced a sound weld under a specific Canadian standard, with a specific process, in a specific position, while someone from the Canadian Welding Bureau watched. On the shop floor it is called a "ticket", and it comes with a wallet card that lists what you are qualified for.

The requirement does not come from a provincial apprenticeship rule. It comes from the work. Welders on certain products, buildings and bridges in Canada must be qualified by the CWB under one of the CSA welder qualification standards, according to the CWB's welder qualification page. If a fabricator is certified to build structural steel, the people welding on that steel need current tickets for the processes and positions they actually use.

In practice, that covers most structural, miscellaneous metals, heavy equipment, trailer, tank and pressure-adjacent fabrication shops in Canada. Hobby work, farm repair and a lot of maintenance welding fall outside it, but employers still use the ticket as a hiring filter because it is the one credential every Canadian shop recognizes. The CWB describes its qualification as the only nationally recognized welding credential in the country. That is the bureau's own description, but you will rarely meet a Canadian foreman who disagrees.

Which CSA standard applies to you: W47.1, W47.2, W186 or W55.3?

The standard on your ticket depends on the material you weld. Four CSA standards cover welder qualification through the CWB, and a fifth one, W59, is often confused with them.

StandardWhat it coversTypical work
CSA W47.1Certification of companies for fusion welding of steelStructural steel, misc. metals, equipment, trailers
CSA W47.2Certification of companies for fusion welding of aluminumTruck bodies, marine, railings, platforms
CSA W186Welding of reinforcing bars in reinforced concrete constructionRebar splices and assemblies
CSA W55.3Resistance weldingSpot and projection welding in production
CSA W59 (not a qualification standard)Design and fabrication of welded steel constructionSets the weld rules the work must meet

The distinction between W47.1 and W59 matters when you read a job posting. W47.1 is about who is allowed to weld and how companies and welders are certified. W59 is the design and fabrication code for welded steel, covering joint details, workmanship and inspection. The CWB explains that the two standards together define what a company welding steel has to do. W59.2 plays the same role for aluminum.

So when a posting says "W59 experience", it is asking whether you know the fabrication rules. When it says "W47.1 ticket, all-position FCAW", it is asking what is on your card. If you work mostly in the United States or for US clients, you will see AWS D1.1 instead, which we cover in AWS D1.1 acceptance criteria explained.

How do you get a CWB ticket? The three routes

There are three ways to sit a CWB welder qualification test, and the one you pick changes the cost, the paperwork and how portable the ticket is afterward.

Route 1: Test through a CWB-certified employer

This is the most common route. Your employer is certified under W47.1 or W47.2, has approved welding procedures (WPS and WPDS), and books a CWB representative to witness tests in the shop. You weld the test plate to the company's procedure, on the company's equipment, usually on company time. The employer normally covers the fee.

The trade-off is that your qualification is tied to that company's procedures and your continued employment there. If you leave, the ticket has to be transferred to another certified company within a set period or it lapses (more on that below).

Route 2: Test at a CWB Accredited Test Centre

Accredited test centres are usually colleges, private welding schools or the CWB's own centre in Nisku, Alberta. You pay the fee yourself, weld to the test centre's procedures, and leave with a qualification you can show a new employer. This is the route for students, welders between jobs and anyone who wants a ticket before applying.

Book early. Popular centres run scheduled sessions, and a spring session near a college intake can fill weeks in advance.

Route 3: Test through a CWB Accredited Union Hall

Some union halls are accredited to run CWB testing for their members. If you are in a building trades local, this is often the cheapest and most convenient path, and the hall usually knows exactly which tickets its contractors are asking for this season.

What happens on test day

Whichever route you use, the test is practical. You weld a test assembly to an approved procedure while a CWB Certification Services Representative watches. The representative stamps the assembly with identification so specimens can be traced back to you. After you finish, the plate is cut, prepared and bent, and the CWB evaluates the results.

Bring your own helmet, gloves, jacket, safety glasses, hearing protection and boots. Most centres supply the machine, plate and filler. Ask in advance whether you can bring your own grinder and wire brushes, because some centres prefer you use theirs.

Which processes and classifications can you test for?

You test one process at a time. The CWB qualifies welders in SMAW (stick), FCAW and MCAW (flux-cored and metal-cored), GMAW (MIG), SAW (submerged arc), GTAW (TIG) and ESW/EGW (electroslag and electrogas).

Each qualification also carries a classification letter. This is the part new welders most often get wrong when they read a job posting, so it is worth learning.

ClassWhat you are qualified to weldAlso covers
FWFillet welds and tack welds—
SFull-thickness groove welds from both sides, or from one side with backingFW
TFull-thickness groove welds from one side without backingS and FW

A "T" ticket is the hardest to earn because you have to make a sound root with nothing behind it. It is also the most valuable, because it covers everything below it. If a posting asks for "S" and you hold "T", you are covered. The reverse is not true.

Which positions should you test in?

Welds are tested in the flat, horizontal, vertical and overhead positions, and plate and pipe tests are separate. The test sheet for a common steel plate test, a 10 mm (3/8") single-V groove butt joint welded with E7018, lists flat, horizontal, vertical-up and overhead. CSA W47.1 sets out which positions each test qualifies you for, and that detail changes between editions of the standard, so confirm it with your test centre before you book.

The practical advice is simpler: test in the positions you will be paid to weld. Structural erection work needs vertical and overhead. A shop that welds everything on positioners may only need flat and horizontal. If you are not sure, ask the hiring foreman which positions they expect, then book those.

How is a CWB test plate evaluated? The bend test

The CWB judges most plate tests by bending specimens cut from your weld until they fold back on themselves. A weld with lack of fusion, slag or porosity opens up on the outside of the bend. A sound weld stretches without cracking.

The procedure is set out in CWB Form 117E, the guide for preparing and bend testing welder test specimens. In short:

  1. The specimen locations are marked on the test assembly, and the discard is removed from each end.
  2. The backing bar, if any, is removed completely without taking away base metal.
  3. The assembly is straightened and the weld reinforcement is ground flush with the plate.
  4. Specimens are cut to size, each keeping the identification stamped by the CWB representative.
  5. Edges get a 3 mm corner radius and a final belt-sanding perpendicular to the weld.
  6. Each specimen is bent through 180° in a plunger-and-die or wrap-around jig built to the dimensions in the standard.

The number and type of bends depend on the material, thickness and standard.

Test assemblyCSA W47.1 (steel)CSA W47.2 (aluminum)
Plate 10 mm or thinner2 root bends, 1 face bend2 root bends, 2 face bends, 2 fractures, 2 macro-etch
Plate thicker than 10 mm2 root bends, 1 face bend4 side bends, 4 fractures, 2 macro-etch
PipePer Figure 13 of W47.1Per Figure 24 of W47.2

Source: CWB Form 117E/2023-2. Stainless steel (Annex K) and other annexes have their own specimen rules.

Two warnings from the same guide. Specimens that show signs of excessive grinding are treated as failures, so do not try to "help" a coupon by grinding below the plate surface. And tampering, such as rewelding to hide a defect or dressing a bent specimen, means immediate failure and loss of bend testing privileges for the site.

Why most bend tests fail

The usual causes are a short list:

  • Lack of fusion at the root. Usually from running too cold on the root, or not getting into the corner of the backing bar. It shows up on the root bend.
  • Cold lap on the cap. A cap laid on top of the fill without wetting into the toes. It shows up on the face bend.
  • Slag lines between passes. Common with stick and FCAW when inter-pass cleaning is rushed, especially in the overhead position.
  • Restarts. A cold restart in the middle of the coupon is a classic failure point. Grind your stops and restart ahead of the crater.
  • Porosity from wet electrodes or drafts. Keep low-hydrogen rods in the oven until you need them, and close the booth curtain.

The fix is boring: practise the exact joint, thickness, process and position you will test on, and bend your own practice coupons. A cheap hydraulic bender at a school or a friendly shop will tell you more in an afternoon than a week of welding practice plates you never cut.

How much does a CWB test cost in 2026?

There is no national price list. The CWB charges the certification fees, and each test centre sets its own per-position price on top for plate, booth time and administration. The CWB's fee notices make clear that test centre pricing is separate.

Budget for more than the fee. A realistic all-in figure for someone testing independently includes:

  • Test fees for each position, about $170–$185 each, so roughly $700 for a four-position plate ticket.
  • Practice time. Some centres sell booth time by the hour or by the plate. Two or three practice sessions before the real test are normal.
  • A retest if you fail a position. You can retest within 90 days, but you pay again.
  • Travel and lost wages for the test day.
  • Your own PPE if it is worn out. Test centres will turn you away without proper boots and a jacket. A new auto-darkening helmet and FR jacket from the welding helmets section of the HubWeld shop is often cheaper than one failed position.

If you are employed by a certified company, the employer normally pays. If you are a student, ask your college whether the first ticket is included in tuition. Some programs build it in.

How long is a CWB ticket valid and how do you renew it?

Most CWB welder qualifications are valid for two years, provided you stay continuously employed by a CWB-certified company. Some qualifications have shorter periods, and some do not expire. The rules below come from the CWB's welder qualification page and apply to most tickets.

  • Check test window. You can renew with a check test from 90 days before to 90 days after the expiry date. Miss that window and you sit a full test again.
  • Failed test. You can retest within 90 days.
  • Inactivity. A qualification can be revoked if you have not worked with that process for three months or more.
  • Changing jobs. If your card is transferable and you are not rehired by a certified company within three months of leaving, it can be revoked.
  • Lost card. A replacement wallet card is reissued through your company's certification contact, not directly to you.

Put the expiry date in your phone calendar the day you get the card, with a reminder 90 days out. Letting a ticket lapse because nobody booked the check test is the most avoidable cost in this trade.

Step-by-step: getting your first CWB ticket

If you are starting from zero, this is the order that works for most people.

Step 1: Decide which ticket you need

Look at ten real job postings in your area for the kind of work you want. Write down the process, classification and positions they ask for. In structural fabrication, FCAW and SMAW plate tickets are the usual ask, often with vertical and overhead positions. Pipe and TIG tickets matter more in industrial maintenance and process piping. Our article on welding jobs in Canada in 2026 shows where demand sits by province and sector.

Step 2: Build the skill before you pay for the test

A college welding program, a pre-apprenticeship course or time on the floor as a helper will get you there. If you are self-taught, take a short CWB prep course at a test centre. They will run you on the exact plate and procedure you will be tested on. For process fundamentals, our MIG welding for beginners guide and the stick electrode guide cover settings and technique.

Step 3: Pick your route and book

If you have an employer, ask the shop's certification contact to put you on the next test day. If not, call a CWB Accredited Test Centre, a college or your union hall. Ask four questions:

  1. Which standard, process and plate thickness will I be tested on?
  2. What is the price per position, and is there a package rate?
  3. Can I book practice time on the same plate before the test?
  4. Do I keep the qualification if I change employers, and how is it transferred?

Step 4: Practise the actual test

Weld the test joint at least a dozen times. Cut and bend your own coupons. Practise the hardest position until it is boring. For most people that means overhead with E7018 or vertical-up FCAW. Time yourself, because test days have time limits.

Step 5: Test day

Arrive early, check your machine settings on a scrap plate if allowed, and do not rush the root. Clean every pass properly. Grind stops and starts. Ask the representative before you do anything you are unsure about, such as grinding a tack or replacing a contact tip mid-test.

Step 6: After the test

Results come from the CWB once specimens are evaluated. When your card arrives, photograph both sides, note the expiry date and update every place an employer might look: your resume, your LinkedIn and your HubWeld welder profile.

CWB ticket vs Red Seal: what is the difference?

A CWB ticket proves you can make a sound weld with one process in specific positions, today. A Red Seal proves you completed a full welding apprenticeship and passed an interprovincial exam covering layout, fabrication, cutting, gouging and welding processes. They answer different questions, which is why many employers ask for both.

Beyond the welder ticket: CWB welding inspector certification

Once you have a few years on tickets, the next step for many welders is inspection. The CWB certifies welding inspectors under CSA W178.2 at three levels, according to the CWB inspector certification requirements:

  • Level 1 is the entry level.
  • Level 2 requires at least two years as a certified Level 1 inspector.
  • Level 3 requires at least 48 months as a certified Level 2 inspector.

Every level requires a practical exam, a theory exam, a vision test and agreement to a code of ethics. Inspection work pairs well with knowledge of non-destructive testing, covered in weld inspection 101, and with reading procedures, covered in how to read a WPS.

A separate but related body, the CWB Association, is a free membership organization within CWB Group that reports over 70,000 members. Membership costs nothing, and it gives you access to the association's events and technical resources.

Common mistakes welders make with CWB tickets

Most ticket problems are paperwork problems, not welding problems.

  • Testing for the wrong thing. A flat-and-horizontal GMAW ticket does not help you on a site that needs overhead stick. Match the ticket to the work.
  • Assuming the ticket follows you. If you tested in-house and then left, check whether your qualification can be transferred and how long you have.
  • Letting the check test slip. The window closes 90 days after expiry. After that, you pay for and sit a full test.
  • Overstating the card. Write the exact process, class and positions on your resume. Employers check, and a mismatch costs you the job.
  • Not practising on the test plate. Practising on 1/4" plate and testing on 3/8" with a backing bar is a different weld.

Frequently asked questions

How long does it take to get CWB certified?

The test itself takes a day or less. Getting ready takes longer. A welder with shop experience may need a few practice sessions on the test plate. Someone starting from scratch usually completes a college program or pre-apprenticeship first, which takes several months. Results come from the CWB after the bend specimens are evaluated.

Can I get a CWB ticket without an employer?

Yes. You can test at a CWB Accredited Test Centre, such as a college or the CWB's own centre in Nisku, Alberta, or through a CWB Accredited Union Hall if you are a member. You pay the fee yourself. Ask how the qualification is maintained and transferred once you are hired by a certified company.

How much does a CWB welding test cost in Canada?

Test centres set their own prices. Published 2026 prices are about $170 to $185 per position, for example $180 per position at SAE Estrie in Quebec and $180 to $185 at New Brunswick Community College, which caps a four-position package at $720. Add practice time, travel and any retests.

How often do you have to renew a CWB ticket?

Most CWB qualifications are valid for two years while you remain continuously employed by a certified company. You renew with a check test, which you can take from 90 days before to 90 days after expiry. After that window you need a full test. Some qualifications have shorter periods and some do not expire.

What does the CWB bend test check?

Specimens cut from your test weld are ground flush and bent 180 degrees in a jig. Lack of fusion, slag, porosity or cracking opens on the outside of the bend. For steel plate under CSA W47.1, the test uses two root bends and one face bend. Over-ground or tampered specimens are failed.

Is a CWB ticket the same as a Red Seal?

No. A CWB ticket qualifies you for a specific process, classification and positions, and usually needs renewal every two years. A Red Seal is a trade certification earned through an apprenticeship and an interprovincial exam, and it does not expire. Many employers ask for both.

Does a CWB ticket let me work in the United States?

Not automatically. US structural work is normally qualified to AWS D1.1, and the employer or inspector decides which qualification they accept. Some Canadian shops working on US projects qualify welders to both. Ask the client before the job starts.
