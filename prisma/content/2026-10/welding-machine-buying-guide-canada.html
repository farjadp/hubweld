The short version of any welding machine buying guide for Canada: work through these questions in order: the metal and thickness you weld, the process, the power you actually have (120 V, 240 V single-phase, or 600 V three-phase in most plants), amperage and duty cycle, portability, a recognized Canadian certification mark, and parts and service support here. Budget in CAD and add GST/HST or PST to the price. Expect roughly $1,200 to $3,500 for a capable 120/240 V shop machine.

Key takeaways

  • Buy for the thickest metal you weld every week, not the thickest you might weld once. That single number sets your amperage and your input power.
  • Canadian industrial plants usually run 600 V three-phase (often labelled 575 V on welders), not the 480 V common in US plants. A US-spec 208/230/460 V machine may need a transformer.
  • Electrical equipment sold or used in Ontario must carry the mark of a certification body accredited by the Standards Council of Canada, or a field-evaluation label. The current arc welding safety standard is CSA C22.2 No. 60974-1.
  • Duty cycle is rated at a stated amperage. "200 A at 30%" means 3 minutes of arc in every 10 at 200 A, with cooling time for the rest.
  • Sales tax runs from 5% GST in Alberta and the territories to 15% HST in New Brunswick, Newfoundland and Labrador and PEI. Price it in before you compare quotes.
  • A machine with no Canadian service centre or parts stock is a gamble. Check where a drive roll, torch liner or control board comes from before you pay.

Most welder buying guides online are written for the US market. They quote prices in US dollars, assume 480 V three-phase, and skip the question every Canadian inspector and insurer eventually asks: does it carry an approved mark? This guide is the Canadian version. It walks through the decision the way a shop foreman would, one step at a time, and links to the parts of the HubWeld shop and blog that go deeper on each step.

Step 1: decide what you will weld and how thick it is

The first question to answer is the metal and its thickness, because that answer fixes everything after it. A machine that welds 18-gauge sheet well is a different tool from one that runs 1/2 in. structural plate all shift.

Write down three things before you look at a single product page:

  1. Base metals. Mild steel only? Stainless? Aluminum? Aluminum TIG needs AC output, which rules out DC-only TIG machines. Aluminum MIG works best with a spool gun or a push-pull gun.
  2. Typical thickness. The metal you weld most weeks. If 90% of your work is 1/8 in. to 1/4 in. steel, size for 1/4 in. and accept multiple passes on the occasional thicker job.
  3. Where you weld. In a shop, on a trailer, on a farm, on a site with no power, or outside in wind? Wind blows shielding gas away, which pushes outdoor work toward stick or self-shielded flux-core.

Use the table below as a starting point. The single-pass figures are the kind of ratings manufacturers print for their machines. Treat them as rough limits: joint design, bevel prep and technique all move the number.

Step 2: pick the welding process

The process you choose decides the machine family, and the right one depends on Step 1. Here is the short version. For the full comparison, read our guide to MIG vs TIG vs stick welding.

  • MIG (GMAW). Solid wire with shielding gas. Fast, clean and the easiest process to learn. The default for shop fabrication on steel. Needs a gas cylinder and shelter from wind. If you are new to it, start with our MIG welding for beginners guide.
  • Flux-core (FCAW). Same machine as MIG, with tubular wire. Self-shielded wire works outdoors without a gas bottle. Gas-shielded (dual shield) wire is a production process for thicker structural work. Our article on flux-cored welding pros and cons covers the trade-offs.
  • Stick (SMAW). The simplest machine and the most forgiving of rust, paint and wind. Slow, with slag to chip. Still the backbone of field repair and a lot of pipe work.
  • TIG (GTAW). The most control and the cleanest welds, at the slowest travel speed. Needed for thin stainless, aluminum (with AC) and critical root passes.
  • Multi-process. One power source that runs MIG, flux-core, stick and DC TIG, and in some models AC TIG. Good for small shops and mobile welders who need flexibility more than peak output.

Step 3: match the machine to the power you have

Input power is where Canadian buyers most often get caught, because Canadian and US electrical systems differ in ways that matter for welders. Check the power at your outlet or panel before you choose a model, not after it arrives.

120 V vs 240 V welders

A 120 V welder plugs into a standard household outlet. That convenience comes with a hard limit. A typical 15 A or 20 A household circuit can only feed so much power into the arc, so 120 V MIG machines top out around 140 A of output. That is enough for sheet metal and light repair, and not much more.

A 240 V single-phase welder roughly doubles what you can draw from the wall, which is why 200 to 250 A machines need it. Many of them come with a 50 A, 250 V plug. The common type is the NEMA 6-50, which welder makers such as Hobart sell as a matching wall receptacle. Your garage may have a 240 V dryer or range outlet, but those use different plug configurations and often a different circuit size. Do not adapt your way around that.

Dual-voltage machines handle both. They usually ship with a 120 V plug and an adapter or swappable cord for 240 V. For most small shops and mobile welders they are the sensible middle ground: light work on any outlet, full output when 240 V is available.

Three-phase power: 600 V in Canada, 480 V in the US

Most Canadian industrial buildings are fed with 600 V three-phase power. Fluke's support notes put it plainly: typical three-phase systems in Canada are 600 V, while the US standard is 480 V, and the leg-to-ground voltage on a 600 V system is 347 V. Welder nameplates often call this 575 V. It is the same supply class.

This matters when you buy industrial machines. Many large power sources are built in separate voltage versions, and the 575 V version is the one you want for a Canadian plant. The HubWeld catalogue lists several machines in 575 V configurations, from multiprocess inverters to pipe systems. A US-market 208/230/460 V unit can work in Canada, but only on a 208 V or 240 V supply, or behind a step-down transformer on 600 V. That transformer is an extra cost and another piece of gear to install and inspect.

Smaller shops often have 120/208 V three-phase service. Plenty of inverters accept 208 V to 230 V three-phase, which gives smoother power draw than single-phase. Read the input range on the spec sheet, not just the headline voltage.

Wiring, breakers and the electrical code

Welders are covered by their own section of the Canadian Electrical Code. Section 42 deals with electric welders: supply conductor sizing based on duty cycle, receptacle ratings for cord-connected welders, and disconnects. The 2021 edition extended the transformer-welder rules to inverter welders. Practically, that means:

  • Give your electrician the welder's rated primary current and duty cycle from the nameplate. Those numbers size the wire and the breaker, not the plug alone.
  • A new 240 V or three-phase circuit needs an electrical permit and inspection in most provinces. In Ontario, that is the Electrical Safety Authority.
  • Generators: inverter welders can run from a generator, but check the manufacturer's minimum generator size. An undersized generator causes erratic arcs and can damage electronics.

Step 4: size the amperage and duty cycle

Amperage sets how thick you can weld. Duty cycle sets how long you can keep welding at that amperage. Both appear on every spec sheet, and the duty cycle number is the one buyers misread most often.

Duty cycle is the percentage of a 10-minute period a machine can weld at a stated output before it needs to cool. A rating of 200 A at 30% means 3 minutes of arc and 7 minutes of cooling, at 200 A. At lower amperage the duty cycle rises. A manufacturer that prints only "up to 60% duty cycle" without the amperage is telling you very little.

Rules of thumb that hold up in practice:

  • Hobby and repair: 20% to 30% at your working amperage is usually fine. You stop to fit, grind and tack anyway.
  • Fabrication shop: look for 60% or better at the amperage you run most.
  • Production and automation: 100% at working amperage, which usually means an industrial three-phase machine.

Our article on duty cycle and sizing a welder works through the arithmetic with examples, including how ambient temperature changes the picture. One Canadian note: duty cycle tests are run at a set ambient temperature. A machine in a hot, closed summer shop runs out of duty cycle sooner. A machine in a cold shop in January is more limited by your fingers than its fan.

Wire size and electrode size

Match the amperage range to the filler you plan to run. A 120 V MIG will feed 0.023 in. and 0.030 in. wire well and 0.035 in. at its limit. A 250 A machine runs 0.035 in. and 0.045 in. comfortably. For stick, a 3/32 in. E7018 runs around 70 to 110 A and a 1/8 in. around 90 to 160 A, depending on position and the manufacturer's data. If you plan to run 5/32 in. rod, you need a machine that can hold 150 A or more at a useful duty cycle. Our stick electrode guide covers classifications and amperage ranges.

Step 5: decide how portable the machine needs to be

Portability splits buyers into three groups: those who never move the machine, those who move it between jobs, and those who weld where there is no power at all. Each group should buy differently.

Inverter vs transformer machines

Inverter welders use electronics to convert power, which makes them a fraction of the weight of older transformer machines and more efficient on the input side. A 200 A inverter can weigh less than 25 kg. Older transformer machines are heavy and simple, and many have run for decades. If the machine lives on a cart in one shop, weight matters less than parts support. If it rides in a truck, inverter technology is the obvious choice.

Engine-driven welders

If you weld on farms, pipelines, construction sites or anywhere without reliable power, you need an engine drive: a welder and generator built around a gas or diesel engine. Smaller gasoline units in the 260 A class handle stick and flux-core repair and power your grinder from the auxiliary outlets. Larger diesel units in the 400 A class run longer shifts, multiple processes, and in some models an air compressor for gouging.

Questions to answer before buying an engine drive:

  • Gas or diesel? Gasoline is cheaper to buy. Diesel costs more upfront and pays it back in fuel economy and engine life if the machine runs most days.
  • Auxiliary power. How many kilowatts does it supply while welding? That runs grinders, lights and a heater on a cold site.
  • Cold starting. Canadian winters are hard on engines. Ask about block heaters, battery size and the engine maker's cold-start guidance.
  • Truck mounting. Weight, footprint and lifting points decide whether it fits your truck deck or service body.

You can compare current engine drives in the HubWeld shop, linked at the end of this guide. If you run a mobile welding business, our piece on growing a mobile welding business covers how equipment choice ties into what you can bill.

Step 6: check for a Canadian certification mark

A CSA certified welder, or one carrying another recognized Canadian mark, is not a nice-to-have. In Ontario it is the law for selling and using electrical products. Under Ontario Regulation 438/07, no one may use, advertise, display or sell an electrical product unless it has been approved: certified by a certification body accredited by the Standards Council of Canada and recognized by the Electrical Safety Authority, with that body's mark on the product, or carrying a field evaluation label. Other provinces have comparable rules.

What to look for on the machine and its nameplate:

  • A mark valid for Canada. The CSA mark is the familiar one. Marks from other SCC-accredited bodies are also valid when they include the Canadian indicator, such as UL's "c" or "C-US" marking or Intertek's ETL mark with the "c". A US-only mark is not enough.
  • The right standard. Arc welding power sources are now certified to CSA C22.2 No. 60974-1, which is harmonized with the international IEC 60974-1. The older CSA C22.2 No. 60 has been withdrawn. You will still see it in older literature and on older machines.
  • A nameplate you can read. Input voltage, phase, rated primary current, output range and duty cycle. Your electrician needs these numbers.

Why it matters beyond the regulation: an uncertified machine can be refused by an inspector, flagged in an insurance claim after a fire, or turned away at a client's site. Cheap grey-market imports bought online are the usual culprits. If a listing doesn't say which mark the machine carries, ask the seller in writing.

Step 7: check warranty, parts and service in Canada

A welder is only as good as its supply of wear parts and its repair path. Before you buy, find out how you would get the machine fixed in Canada and how fast you can get consumables for it.

Ask these questions:

  1. Who honours the warranty in Canada? Some brands warranty the machine only when bought from an authorized Canadian dealer. A machine shipped across the border may not be covered.
  2. Where is the nearest authorized service centre? Shipping a 30 kg inverter to another province for a board swap costs time and money.
  3. Are the wear parts standard? Contact tips, nozzles, liners and drive rolls should be common types you can buy anywhere. A proprietary torch with hard-to-find tips becomes a problem on the second box. Browse tips and nozzles or drive rolls and liners to see what is carried for the brand you are considering.
  4. Does the gun use a standard connection? Common MIG gun connections let you swap to a third-party gun later. Proprietary ones lock you in.

Parts availability also decides how long the machine stays useful. Shops still running decades-old transformer machines do it because parts exist. If you buy used, check that first. Our playbook on sourcing obsolete welding parts explains what to do when they don't.

Step 8: set a budget in Canadian dollars

Budget for the whole setup, with the power source as one line of several, and include sales tax. Canadian prices differ from US prices for reasons beyond the exchange rate: distribution, certification, warranty support and, as of 2026, tariffs that have pushed up costs for steel and some steel products.

First-time buyers often forget the rest of the kit, which can add 20% to 40% to a small setup:

Step 9: decide where to buy a welder in Canada

You have four realistic places to buy: a local welding supply store, a Canadian online retailer or marketplace, a big-box store, or the used market. Each has a place.

  • Local welding supply store. Hands-on demos, advice, a gas supply relationship and someone to talk to when something breaks. Often the best choice for your first serious machine.
  • Online in Canada. Wider selection and easier price comparison. Prices should be in CAD, include certification information, and say who handles warranty. Buying welding supplies online in Canada explains how ordering on HubWeld works, including tax, shipping and payment.
  • Big-box stores. Fine for entry-level 120 V machines. Limited choice above that, and staff usually can't help with setup.
  • Used. Good value on industrial machines, with risk. Test it under load at the amperage you need, check the nameplate and certification mark, and confirm that parts are still available.

Avoid cross-border orders unless you have checked three things: the machine carries a mark valid in Canada, the warranty applies here, and you know the total landed cost. Duties, brokerage fees, tariffs and the exchange rate can erase the price gap quickly.

Pre-purchase checklist for buying a welder in Canada

Run through this list before you pay for any welding machine. If you cannot tick an item, find the answer first.

  1. I know the base metals and the thickness I weld most weeks.
  2. I have chosen the process (MIG, flux-core, stick, TIG or multi-process) for that work.
  3. I have confirmed the input power at the outlet or panel: 120 V, 240 V single-phase, 208 V three-phase or 600 V three-phase.
  4. The machine's input voltage and phase match my supply, including a 575 V version for a 600 V plant.
  5. The output range covers my typical amperage, with some headroom.
  6. The duty cycle at my working amperage suits the way I work.
  7. The machine carries a certification mark valid in Canada (CSA, or another SCC-accredited body's mark with the Canadian indicator).
  8. I know who honours the warranty in Canada and where the nearest service centre is.
  9. Wear parts (tips, nozzles, liners, drive rolls) are standard and available.
  10. I have budgeted for the helmet, PPE, gas, regulator, consumables and any electrical work.
  11. I have added the correct provincial sales tax to every quote I am comparing.
  12. For an engine drive, I have checked cold-start equipment and truck mounting.

Common mistakes when buying a welder

The same mistakes come up again and again with Canadian buyers. Most of them cost more to fix than they would have cost to avoid.

  • Buying for the rare job. Paying for 400 A when 95% of your work needs 180 A. Rent or subcontract the occasional heavy job instead.
  • Ignoring input power. Ordering a 240 V machine for a garage with only 120 V service, or a 460 V US unit for a 600 V plant.
  • Reading duty cycle without the amperage. Comparing "60%" on one machine with "30%" on another without checking the output each figure is rated at.
  • Skipping the certification mark. An unapproved machine can be refused by an inspector or a client and can complicate an insurance claim.
  • Forgetting consumable costs. A cheap machine with proprietary tips and liners costs more over three years than a pricier one with standard parts.
  • Comparing pre-tax and tax-in prices. A quote from another province or an all-in price from a store can look cheaper than it is.

Frequently asked questions

What is the best welder to buy in Canada for a beginner?

For most beginners, a dual-voltage 120/240 V MIG or multi-process machine in the 140 to 220 A range is the best first welder. It runs from a household outlet for light work and from a 240 V circuit for thicker steel. Make sure it carries a mark valid in Canada, such as CSA, and that its tips and liners are standard parts you can buy locally.

Can I use a 120 V welder on a regular outlet?

Yes. A 120 V welder is designed for a standard household outlet, ideally on its own 20 A circuit. Output is limited to roughly 140 A, which suits sheet metal, auto body and light repair on steel up to about 3/16 in. in one pass. Avoid long, thin extension cords, which drop voltage and give a poor arc. Check the manufacturer's recommended circuit and cord size.

Do welders sold in Canada need CSA certification?

They need approval from a recognized certification body, which is not only CSA. In Ontario, Regulation 438/07 says an electrical product must carry the mark of a body accredited by the Standards Council of Canada and recognized by ESA, or a field evaluation label, before it can be sold or used. UL and Intertek marks with the Canadian indicator also qualify.

Will a US welder work in Canada?

Single-phase 120 V and 240 V machines generally work, since household voltages are the same. Three-phase industrial machines are different: Canadian plants usually run 600 V, while US plants run 480 V. A 208/230/460 V US unit needs a 208 V or 240 V supply, or a transformer on 600 V. Also confirm the machine carries a Canadian mark and that its warranty applies in Canada.

What does 575 V mean on a welder?

575 V is the nameplate rating for welders built for Canada's 600 V three-phase industrial power. It is the same supply class as 600 V. Many industrial power sources are sold in separate 575 V versions for the Canadian market. If your plant has 600 V three-phase service, a 575 V machine connects directly without a step-down transformer.

How much should I spend on my first welding machine in Canada?

Budget roughly $1,200 to $3,500 CAD for a capable 120/240 V machine, then add 20% to 40% for a helmet, PPE, gas cylinder, regulator and consumables. Add provincial sales tax, from 5% in Alberta to 15% in Atlantic provinces. Spending more makes sense if you need higher duty cycle, AC TIG for aluminum, or three-phase power.

Should I buy a gas or diesel engine-driven welder?

Buy gasoline if the machine runs a few hours a week and price matters most. Buy diesel if it runs most working days, since diesel engines use less fuel and last longer. In Canada, also check cold-start equipment, such as block heaters and battery capacity, and how many kilowatts of auxiliary power the machine supplies while you weld.
